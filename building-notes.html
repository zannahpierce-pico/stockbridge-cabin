Notes on fit

  • Couch rug: 8' is 5" wider than the floor under the loft, so it runs that far past the loft edge toward the kitchen. A 7'-6" square would stay inside the loft edge. The front edge of an 8' rug ends at the foot of the ladder and about 3'-6" from the centre of the wood stove; check the stove's label for floor protection.
  • Couch rug and kitchen rug: the kitchen rug ends about 1" before the couch rug begins, and their corners line up for about 10".
  • Kitchen rug: leaves about 4" of floor each side in the aisle. It stops about 1'-8" short of the end of the counter and 2'-8" short of the first stool.
  • Loft rug: 7'-6" leaves about 1" in a 7'-7" loft, and the railing posts take about 1 1/2" of that at floor level. The rug has to slide under the railing's bottom rail and may need to curl slightly at the posts. Measure wall to railing post before ordering; rugs also run an inch or two off their stated size. If it is too tight, 6' x 9' is the next size down.
  • Loft rug: keep the front edge at least 1' back from where the ladder steps off, and use a rug pad.

  • The Rinnai is the quietest and most even, but it needs power all the time it runs: about half a kilowatt-hour a day from the battery. The other two need no electricity at all, which suits a battery cabin and a power-off freeze guard.
  • Where one could go:
    • Front wall, under the big fixed window, beside the wood stove's hearth (where the wood box is drawn). The sill is 25" up. On size alone the Empire fits under it, but the Martin does not once its clearances are counted (see below), and the Empire's clearances have not been checked. The Rinnai is too tall.
    • Bank wall, in the back corner under the mirror. Any of the three fit below the mirror. Outside is the bank and the retaining wall, close to the corner: check the vent has room.
    • Bank wall between the couch and the low window. Only about 16" of wall: the Martin fits, the Empire barely, the Rinnai not. Close to the couch.
    • Porch wall between the bench and the slider. Under the tall windows (sill 24 1/2"): the Empire or Martin fit. It would vent onto the covered deck: check that is allowed.
  • Every position needs the maker's clearances checked: to the floor, to things above and beside it, and for the vent outside (distance from opening windows, doors, the deck, the ground and snow).
  • Not suggested: unvented heaters (blue-flame or catalytic, including the portable "Buddy" type). They put moisture and combustion gases into a small, tight room, and many are not approved for sleeping rooms.
  • If cooking also goes to propane, one tank and regulator outside could serve both.

Martin MDV8P: looked into further (2026-10-07)

Easy to buy in the USA: several off-grid and cabin suppliers list it in stock, at about $640 to $930 (an earlier note here that it was discontinued was wrong; that came from one Canadian distributor's page). Details below are from the owner's manual, which covers the MDV8, MDV12 and MDV20.

  • Heat: 8,000 BTU in, 6,400 out, turning down to 2,900. Sellers rate it for about 194 sq ft. The MDV12 is the next size up: 11,000 in, 8,800 out.
  • No electricity. Standing pilot lit with a push-button igniter, a HI to LO thermostat knob, and a safety that shuts the gas if the pilot goes out. So it can be left on low as a freeze guard.
  • Size: 22 5/8" high x 11 13/16" wide x 7 1/8" deep, about 23 lb.
  • Vent: straight out through the wall directly behind the heater, sloping slightly down to the outside. The vent kit is in the box. The hood sticks out 4 5/16" outside.
  • Wall thickness: must be between 4 1/2" and 12 5/8". CHECK the cabin's walls: the model has them at about 4 1/2", right at the minimum.
  • Clearances to things that burn: the bottom of the heater at least 7" off the floor, 6" each side, 24" above, about 3/4" behind (it stands off the wall on spacers).
  • Vent outside: at least 12" above the ground or a deck, 6" from a window or door that opens, 24" from an outside wall that meets this one at a corner. Under a porch or deck roof it needs local approval.
  • Warranty: one year on parts, lifetime on the combustion chamber, for the original owner; it says installation must be by a licensed technician.
  • Sold in North America by Bismar Inc. of Boucherville, Quebec. Who manufactures it is not stated.
  • Reviews found were for the 20,000 BTU model and were mixed: easy to install and decent heat, but complaints about the thermostat's accuracy and noise.

Where the Martin would fit in this cabin. It needs a patch of outside wall about 24" wide that is clear from 7" to about 54" above the floor (7" + 22 5/8" + 24").

  • It does not fit under the big front window (sill 25"), nor under the tall porch-wall windows (sill 24 1/2"): its top would be about 30" up. This rules out the spot suggested earlier.
  • Back wall, between the red door and the back window: fits. The vent comes out onto the back landing, more than 12" above the deck. This is where the coats, shoe rack and dog crates are drawn, and where the shower would go if the bathroom is built.
  • Bank wall, in the back corner under the mirror: fits if the mirror and the future outlet box above it are kept 24" above the heater's top or moved. Outside is the bank: check the vent is 12" above the ground and clear of snow.
  • Bank wall by the couch or the ladder: too narrow once the 6" side clearances are added.

Other easy-to-buy heaters of the same kind (wall-hung, vented straight through the wall, sold in US farm and hardware shops): the Comfort Glow DVP11 and the Ashley Hearth 11,000 BTU. Both are listed at 20" high x 17 3/8" wide x 6 5/8" deep, about $320 to $440. Whether they need electricity, and their clearances, were not checked.

  • Dickinson Newport Explorer (from the maker's manual): a small stainless propane fireplace with a glass window.
    • Approved for manufactured homes, mobile homes and boats (ANSI Z21.88 / CSA 2.33, CSA 2.17, ABYC A-26). The older P9000 is the marine-only one.
    • The vent must go up through the roof. The manual says horizontal wall exits and 90-degree bends are not possible. The flue is a 1 1/2" pipe inside a 2 1/2" one, between 21" and 56" long in total, straight up if possible, with at most a gradual offset of up to 10". The roof cap works on a pitch up to 20 degrees (this roof is about 14).
    • Because the flue can be no longer than 56", the top of the heater has to be within about 4'-8" of the roof. Under the low side of this ceiling (10'-9") that puts the heater's top about 6' off the floor, and higher anywhere else.
    • Clearances: 20" above it to the ceiling, 20" in front, 6" beneath, 2" each side. The roof cap must be 20" from other vents.
    • Rated for up to about 230 sq ft with a normal 7' to 9' ceiling, well insulated, in a mild climate. This cabin is bigger, taller and in Vermont, so it would be a top-up, not real backup heat.
    • Manual only: no thermostat and no pilot, and the manual says it must never be left running unattended. So it cannot keep the cabin from freezing while nobody is there.
    • Needs a low-pressure regulator and hose, bought separately.
  • Verdict so far: the Explorer is the nicest object, but it needs a hole in the roof (which we were avoiding for the stove pipe), would have to hang high on the wall, and is small for the space. The wall-vented Martin or Empire still fit this cabin better as backup heat.

Glass blocks (top of the cabinet wall)

  • One row between the loft joists, set at the bath face of the wall: 6 blocks, 8" x 8" nominal (7 3/4" actual), 3" to 4" thick, two per gap in three gaps.
  • Each gap is about 21" wide x 9 1/2" high, so wood trim makes up about 3" each side and under 1" top and bottom. Blocks about 6" wide (5 3/4" actual) would fit three per gap (9 blocks) with less trim.
  • Clear, wavy or frosted all work for privacy at that height (6'-8" and up). Acrylic blocks or a silicone-and-spacer kit are easier than mortar here.
  • Leave a soft joint (gasket or sealant) at the top: the loft floor above flexes.
  • The 8" deep ledge left on the living room side takes things up to about 9" tall and 7" deep.

Deck gates (front and back stairs)

Both gates are rolling gates that slide along the deck side of the railing, so the back one never crosses the red door's outward swing.

  • Hardware candidate: Pylex Sliding Gate Hardware Kit, model 11052 (Home Depot, $79.99 on 2026-10-06, black powder-coated steel, 5-year warranty): homedepot.com (one kit per gate, so two).
  • Sizing rule for this kit (from a buyer's review, not the listing): the gate must be 18" longer than the clear opening, and there must be room for that extra length beside the opening.
  • Gates to build (timber frame with balusters to match the railing):
    • Back: 48" clear opening -> 66" gate (5'-6"). Parks toward the porch side, where there is about 7' of railing.
    • Front: about 47" clear opening (one bay between evenly spaced posts, estimated from a photo: measure it) -> about 65" gate (5'-5"). Parks toward the porch side.
  • Materials per gate, scaled from the same review (their 54" gate took two 8' 2x4s and nine 30" balusters): the 66" gate needs about two 8' 2x4s and 11 balusters; the front gate about the same. Plus exterior screws.
  • Tips from that review: pre-drill with a 1/16" bit and go easy on the supplied screws (three snapped for them); it needs a square-drive bit that is not in the box; the gate can take a padlock.
  • NOT CONFIRMED (the listing's spec table is blank): maximum gate width and weight for one kit, though both gates are now close to the 54" example in the review.
